I've been mulling over Issa's thoughts as he's shared them with me
over the years, and also watched several of the videos he has shared
in the post and the linked playlists, as well as some other related
videos. Therefore, although I don't have the first-hand experience
that would give me direct insight into what he is talking about, I do
have a decent sense of the subject matter intellectually. Below are
some of my thoughts (not organized toward one grand thesis or
conclusion, but fitting into a general theme).

### The central ideas of brain retraining are very similar to the ideas prevalent in the exercise world (this can be thought of both as an endorsement, and as a critique of sorts in that "brain retraining" is not that different)

A large part of training for balance, strength, and flexibility is
about neural changes, and specifically CNS changes. Some people even
believe that almost *all* of balance and peak strength improvements
are CNS changes rather than changes to local musculature, though for
peak strength there may be some role for local muscular structure.

The way the changes happen is by "getting the reps in", i.e., doing
enough repetitions of the exercise at the right level of load or
difficulty (progressive overloading), pushing a little bit harder but
not too much.

The role of psychology is understood to mostly be about getting past
the barriers that are preventing one from getting the reps in, and
about getting more value from reps by pushing just past the comfort
zone, rather than as a substitute for getting the reps in. You cannot
just randomly balance on one leg with eyes closed for 5 minutes just
through a psychological shift if you were previously managing only 30
seconds. But if your psychology is getting in the way of you doing the
balance exercise regularly, or if psychology is getting you to give up
at just the 30 second mark rather than trying to push to 35 seconds
even at the cost of discomfort, those are things that are worth
addressing because they are preventing you from getting the reps in.

All of this matches Miguel's concept of progress cycles, as well as a
lot of Issa's description of his own brain retraining experiences,
closely.

### The rare but existent cases of semi-accidental recovery after prolonged illness could partly be about accidental exposure to the right dose enough times as to let the rewiring happen without a conscious shift

It is unlikely but possible to semi-accidentally get exposed to the
right doses that are in the zone where they help rewire without
causing a crash. This could, for instance, be because of one's social
or personal obligations shifting to a level where one has to engage in
some level of activity that's enough to challenge the comfort zone but
not enough to cause a crash. I suspect the Goldilocks problem here
does mean that this kind of semi-accidental recovery after prolonged
illness would be rare, but it's plausible and it might explain some
fraction of the seemingly spontaneous recovery.

### The brain retraining context still poses several challenges different from mainstream exercise

The mainstream exercise world has several advantages:

* The target population is much larger, so there has been a lot more
  ground data informing the development and refinement of protocols
  and concepts. So, the field is much more mature.

* A person starting from a relatively normal physical and cognitive
  baseline (even if somewhat metabolically unhealthy) is facing fewer
  headwinds in general to following a program, in terms of the
  cognitive capacity to comprehend it, the physical ability to do
  auxiliary steps (like get to the gym, go to the store to buy
  equipment), the financial resources to get the equipment or seek
  help, and the social energy to interact with others (in person or
  online) to get feedback. Brain retraining is operating on a target
  population who have diminshed cognitive and physical energy and in
  many cases, because of that, have fewer financial resources and may
  also have fewer actively maintained social connections to draw upon.

* The cost of failure is higher on both sides. Overdoing things can
  lead to crashes, which can set one back by weeks. Going too slowly
  can mean an extra year or two to recover, which is a much bigger
  cost than slower gains in balance or strength over an already
  functional baseline.

* There may be more inter-individual variation (the Anna Karenina
  principle in action) which means there's more need to tailor any
  advice to the individual.

This means that, even if the central idea of brain retraining is
relatively uncontroversial, it may still not be that easy to
implement.

I'm unsure as to the question of whether the brain retraining context
(CFS/ME cluster conditions) have *more* of a role for psychology in a
fundamental sense than exercise training. I don't think there's a good
reason to think this, beyond just the problem being more challenging
and urgent in a sense, so you don't want your psychology working
against you. However, this is a question on which I hold significant
uncertainty.

### Adjustment periods, tactical retreats, and my own exercise experience

Issa's description of the Gupta / Miguel distinction tracks some of my
own impressions both from watching them and from seeing how Issa
responded to them both in real time (unlike Issa, I didn't watch the
full Gupta program, but I watched the free portion of his program and
watched some of his other interviews, so I have a general sense). My
sense is that Gupta's focus is on getting people to a place of
self-acceptance and being gentle, which can be great at the lowest
points. Miguel's progress cycles more closely parallel the exercise
psychology ideas (perhaps unsurprising given Miguel's own fitness
background) and are a better fit once past the lowest points, as one
is charting a recovery course.

On the concept of adjustment period, my read of them is that they
should be treated as "tactical retreats" rather than strategic
withdrawals. Retreat just long enough that the situation "resets"
rather than reading broader implications into it. An interesting
parallel is my own experiences with nociplastic pain in the context of
exercises. I've had periods of maybe a month or two for various
exercises where I had nociplastic pains (they would come and go, would
move around, and did not reliably scale with load). What I did in
those cases was shift the relevant exercises down somewhat without
stopping them completely (reduce the number of reps or target
durations) rather than panic, push through, or give up. A few months
later, I was able to naturally ramp up the level further and continue
my slow and steady upward trajectory.
